## Case 1 — RR Transport: a better question and a better process

*Time: the author’s acting-CEO engagement, 2017–2018, each delivery cycle. Object: the cycle from work order to delivery. Location: RR Transport, a transport and logistics company with 90 trucks; the customer warehouse was about 45 minutes’ drive from head office. The fleet size shows the scale of the company; it is not a result. RR Transport carries AKG products, but this is not an AKG case and not an IT case.*

### Situation

Drivers went to the customer’s warehouse. They loaded a covered van against the shipment or delivery order. Then they drove the loaded van to head office to get advance payment and a fuel slip, and to arrange maintenance. The drive from the warehouse to head office took about 45 minutes. Every step was manual. To monitor a trip, managers asked the driver, “Where are you?”

The author saw that this way of working used up capacity in many places:

- Deliveries were late, and customers were not satisfied.
- Vans used extra fuel.
- Drivers lost time and effort (human energy drain and leakage).
- Fuel records were hard to reconcile with the pump office.
- Vans broke down on the road, and roadside repair took time.
- Records were written by hand in different books. This cost time and effort, and the data were scattered.
- After loading, maintenance sometimes took days. Some goods with expiry dates were damaged because they expired while the loaded van waited.

### What the author did and why

The author changed the question first, and then the process.

**The question.** Monitoring changed from “Where are you?” to “Why are you there?” A service monitoring dashboard and a mobile application followed each job from work order to delivery. GPS tracked each vehicle. The new question needed new evidence: not only where a vehicle was, but why it was there.

**The process.** The author changed the order of the work:

1. The driver comes to the garage, at the head-office location, for a health check of the vehicle.
2. After the check, the driver goes to the warehouse and loads. Then the vehicle goes directly to the customer. The detour to the garage after loading was removed.
3. The driver takes advance money and gets the fuel slip from a manager at the fuel pump. This manager was moved from another job, not newly hired.

**Fuel and records.** The author redesigned fuel management on four principles: economy, efficiency, effectiveness, and value for money. Fuel tickets are issued at the fuel station, and only to assigned vehicles. This reduced false tickets and the gap in reconciliation. Fuel is now reconciled with the pump office every day, like a bank account. The application replaced the handwritten books with a data entry window, so records are made in one place. The need for records stayed. Only the manual writing stopped.

The relevant 5R logic was:

> **RE-ENGINEER** (with Replace of the manual books and a Reduce effect on roadside breakdowns)

The need for advance money, fuel slips, and maintenance was real and stayed. What changed was where and when each was provided.

### Reported result

In the author’s account, changing the question and improving the operating process raised customer satisfaction and the profitability of the business. The author reports the following (author-reported; basis Not recorded):

- profitability restored within 6 months;
- fleet utilization raised to 95–100%;
- service downtime reduced by 80%;
- fewer late deliveries, and less damage to goods, because maintenance is now done before loading;
- lower fuel cost, fewer false fuel tickets, and a smaller reconciliation gap;
- far fewer breakdowns on the road, and far less time spent on roadside repair;
- the recording drain stopped, and decisions became better informed because the data are in one place;
- easier management, because visualization made the operation visible;
- managers and drivers satisfied with the automation (the author’s observation); and
- more trips. The author reports this as the capacity gain: the driver and vehicle time that was freed went into more trips.

**Not known.** The baselines, periods and measurement methods of the figures are Not recorded, and nobody else has verified them. The new question, the new order of work, the health check, fuel control, the application and the dashboard worked together, so their effects cannot be separated. The size of the freed capacity is Not recorded. This record does not assess whether the extra trips were justified or whether future capacity is covered.

### Where to recognize HEE

Context shaped the question. “Where are you?” collects a location and stops. “Why are you there?” collects reasons, and reasons point to causes you can change.

The drain was in the order of the work and in the way of monitoring. It was not in the drivers or managers. The same real needs (cash, fuel slips, maintenance) were met, in a different order and place. So this case is Re-engineer, not Remove: no need was removed, and the causes that kept coming back were changed.

One data window supports what the Decision Point calls Decision Enablement: the people who decide can see current data in one place. This record cannot show that decisions improved. It says only that, in the author’s account, they became better informed.

The health check follows the pattern in Cases 3 and 5: find the problem before it becomes a request or a breakdown, instead of reacting afterwards.

## Case 2 — Bismillah Transport: seeing profit and loss for each vehicle

*Time: a monthly review, 2018–2019, after the RR Transport engagement; the owner’s report is made on the first day of each month. Object: each vehicle’s contribution, and the owner’s decisions about vehicles. Location: Bismillah Transport, a transport and logistics company with 130 trucks. The author was acting CEO during independent advisory work. The fleet size shows the scale of the company; it is not a result. This is not an AKG case and not an IT case.*

### Situation

The owner looked at financial statements. They show how the whole business is doing: its performance and its position. They do not show which vehicles make money and which lose money. A vehicle that loses money every month is hidden inside the total. So the owner’s decisions about vehicles lacked this information.

The author saw that the statements answer “How is the business doing?” but cannot answer “Which vehicles cost the business more than they earn?”

### What the author did and why

The author built an application. It follows the idea of activity-based costing and management (ABC/M), a method the author knows by name and adapted from his own understanding of it; how closely the application matches ABC/M as published has not been checked. It is built around the vehicle:

- The chart of accounts is kept for each vehicle. Maintenance cost is recorded by part (for example engine, tyres, brakes).
- Trips are managed by customer order, so income is tied to a vehicle and an order.
- Maintenance is recorded as planned or unplanned.
- Trip cost, fuel, driver cost, and administrative expense are posted to the vehicle, together with its on-trip or off-trip status.
- Profit or loss for each vehicle is income minus expenses.

The owner makes the report for every vehicle on the first day of each month at 09:00. This is a fixed monthly review.

Nothing was removed in this case. The author gave the owner the evidence to decide. So it is an enablement case, not a drain-removal case.

> **ENABLE** (decision enablement)

### Reported result

In the author’s account, the vehicle-wise profit and loss improved the quality of the owner’s decisions. The owner could see which vehicles lose money every month and are a burden on the business. The financial statements did not show this. The author reports that the owner changed drivers and sold vehicles (author-reported; no counts recorded). The application also supported cost control and income tracking.

For the whole engagement, the author also reports that monthly revenue rose by 35%, and that discipline, accountability, and performance management became stronger (author-reported; baseline and period Not recorded). This record does not attribute these to the vehicle-wise application alone.

**Not known.** No figures are recorded for the vehicles, the drivers changed, or the vehicles sold. The owner’s own reasons are not recorded. The 35% revenue figure is for the whole engagement, not for this change alone. Where any released capacity went is Not recorded. This record does not assess whether the decisions were justified or whether future capacity is covered.

### Where to recognize HEE

Vehicle-wise profit and loss informs the inquiry but does not answer the Two Checks. *Justified?* Was the capacity spent warranted, considering purpose, necessity, proportionality and alternatives? *Covered?* Can credible expected usable capacity meet legitimate next-cycle demand? This record does not document those assessments for individual vehicles.

In the language of the Decision Point, the application gave the owner the information the owner needed, which is Decision Enablement. The owner could then state the reasons for changing a driver or selling a vehicle, and others could review them. That makes the basis of the choice more reviewable. This record does not check the quality of any decision.

## Cases 3 to 8 at Abul Khair Group (AKG)

Cases 3 to 8 are not separate projects with start and end dates. They are one continuing process at AKG, which operates manufacturing businesses that run continuously, 24 hours a day. The author reviewed the IT Support service-request log, found the condition behind recurring requests, changed that condition, and looked again. Each review showed the next condition. This is the order in which the author reports the work unfolded; it is not a prescribed procedure, and no dates are given in this edition.

In the author’s account, the setting shaped the method. IT support has no night shift, so waiting for a request and then reacting was not a workable approach for these problems, and prevention was. This describes Cases 3 to 8, as examples from the IT Support end for end users; it does not describe all of the group’s IT work. Few reactive supports were needed in Cases 3 to 8 (author-reported; no count is recorded). The group’s operations were growing, with new businesses, new users, and new offices. The author aimed at capacity gain and readiness, and prioritised application tuning (input and output), minimising navigation within applications to reduce the time consumed, infrastructure tuning to support the applications, and operation training for IT employees, among related measures (author-reported).

*Self-service* here means the user takes a controlled action, such as running a wizard. *Self-healing* would mean the system corrects itself with no user involved. Cases 3 to 8 use self-service, not self-healing.

For Cases 3 to 8, Time is a continuing process with no dates given, and Location is Abul Khair Group, Bangladesh. The Object of each case is stated in its first line.

## Case 3 — Performance Wizard

*Object: application and desktop slowness requests in the IT Support service-request log.*

### Situation

Users experienced recurring application login-performance problems, desktop slowness, and Windows-related performance issues.

The resulting support demand generated recurring **daily support requests across the relevant problem category**.

The author reviewed three months of the IT Support service-request log, by category, to find the root causes. These included accumulated browser cache and Windows cache.

The conventional response would have been to continue receiving, diagnosing, and resolving these requests.

The intervention instead questioned whether the recurring demand could be addressed differently.

### What the author did and why

A self-service **Performance Wizard** was developed to clean those causes, including browser cache and Windows cache.

The wizard runs on a schedule, and users are asked to run it and check their machine before raising a service request.

The objective was to enable users to perform controlled performance-related actions themselves rather than repeatedly depend on IT personnel for routine intervention.

The primary 5R logic was:

> **REDUCE**

Recurring human-mediated support for these causes was reduced by a controlled self-service mechanism.

### Reported result

The author reports that the scheduled self-service wizard significantly reduced slowness-related requests (author-reported; the measurement basis is Not recorded).

The intervention therefore did not simply improve the speed of request handling.

It changed the demand structure itself.

**Not known.** Request counts are not quantified in this edition, and the size of the reduction is Not recorded. Other changes in the same continuing process, including the hardware changes in Case 5, may have contributed to later reductions. For Cases 3 and 5 together, the author reports that the capacity gained was used to meet continuing user growth (size Not recorded).

### Where to recognize HEE

The case illustrates a central HEE principle:

> **Do not optimize the handling of unnecessary recurring demand before asking whether the demand can be removed, reduced, or replaced.**

The effect sought is not merely fewer tickets.

Reduced support work may create an opportunity to release Human Energy and organizational capacity previously consumed by repetitive support activity. Whether it did depends on how the time was reallocated, any displaced workload, maintenance costs, and subsequent use. The record documents one reported use for Cases 3 and 5 (see [Patterns across the cases](#patterns-across-the-cases)); the other factors are Not recorded.

## Case 4 — RDP / computer restart self-service

*Object: the user-to-IT service chain for computer restarts.*

### Situation

Computer restart requirements generated a recurring service chain:

**User → Local IT → Central IT → Schedule → Action → Notification**

Recurring **daily restart calls across the relevant demand category** were associated with this requirement.

The technical action itself was simple.

The organizational service chain was not.

Human Energy was being consumed by coordination, scheduling, intervention, and notification around a task that could be controlled differently.

### What the author did and why

A self-service restart wizard was introduced.

Users could initiate the required restart through a controlled mechanism without requiring the full support chain.

The primary 5R logic was:

> **REPLACE**

### Reported result

The recurring restart calls were reported to have been reduced to **zero following the intervention** (author-reported).

The important change was therefore not simply faster restart execution.

The recurring human-mediated service chain was removed.

**Not known.** The observation period and supporting records are Not recorded. Whether capacity was freed, and how it was used, is Unknown.

### Where to recognize HEE

This case illustrates that organizational drain can arise from the **service structure surrounding a task**, rather than from the technical complexity of the task itself.

The practical question became:

> **Why should a simple recurring technical action continue to require a multi-step human service chain?**

## Case 5 — Quarterly hardware health check

*Object: hardware-related requests: PC RAM, cooling fans, disks, and connectivity.*

### Situation

Recurring hardware-related problems included:

- blue-screen incidents;
- poor performance;
- dust accumulation;
- connectivity degradation; and
- other hardware conditions capable of generating repeated support demand.

The relevant problem category generated recurring **daily calls**.

The recurring pattern was predominantly reactive.

Problems emerged, users reported them, IT responded, and the cycle repeated.

After the Case 3 wizard, a second review of the service log showed that user PCs, and also printers, monitors, and scanners (mainly PCs), had never been cleaned.

### What the author did and why

A **quarterly preventive hardware health check** was introduced.

The IT Support team was instructed to clean RAM and cooling fans every three months. The checks also detected faulty hardware, mainly RAM and fans, which was replaced. Hard disks were also replaced with solid-state drives, which further reduced performance-related requests. As part of the same operating improvement the author also changed the network architecture (described here only in general terms), and the author reports that a lost-connection problem was traced to how electricity lines had been placed, after which the administration team learned how to place them.

The intervention shifted the operating logic from repeated response toward preventive condition management.

The primary 5R logic was:

> **RE-ENGINEER**

### Reported result

Related recurring calls were reported to have been reduced to **zero following the intervention** (author-reported).

Beyond the call counts, the author reports that users saw how IT Support works, which helped communication, knowledge sharing, and awareness, and that users’ perception of IT service moved from negative to positive (author-observed; no survey or written feedback is recorded).

The intervention therefore changed the conditions generating the demand rather than merely improving the response to incidents.

**Not known.** The observation period and supporting records are Not recorded. The cost of replaced hardware, including the move to solid-state drives, is Not recorded, so no net gain is claimed. Several changes in the same continuing process, including the Case 3 wizard, the drive replacement, and the network change, may have contributed; their separate contributions are Not recorded. For Cases 3 and 5 together, the author reports that the capacity gained was used to meet continuing user growth (size Not recorded).

### Where to recognize HEE

The case illustrates the movement:

> **Response → Prevention**

The relevant HEE question becomes:

> **Why should recurring incidents continue to consume organizational capacity if their underlying conditions can be addressed beforehand?**

Prevention can preserve more capacity than repeated response when recurring problems originate from addressable operating conditions.

## Case 6 — Report performance and information richness

*Object: a recurring report and the information it produces.*

### Situation

A recurring report experienced two related problems.

First, report generation was unnecessarily slow.

Second, the information produced was insufficiently rich and contextual for effective user application.

The report required **minutes** to produce.

The problem was therefore not simply one of technical speed.

It involved the relationship between:

- report structure;
- query and system performance;
- information richness;
- contextual usefulness; and
- system resource consumption.

### What the author did and why

The report was re-engineered across these dimensions.

The intervention addressed:

- report structure;
- query performance;
- system performance;
- information richness;
- contextual usefulness; and
- system health.

The underlying principle was:

> **Question before optimizing.**

Rather than merely trying to make the existing report run faster, the report and its surrounding conditions were reconsidered. The 5R logic was **RE-ENGINEER**.

### Reported result

Report-generation time changed from minutes to seconds:

> **Minutes → seconds**

CPU, RAM, and I/O utilization were brought below the applicable operating threshold, while information usefulness was improved.

**Measurement basis.** The before-and-after observations represent report-generation time under the respective pre- and post-redesign configurations. They are presented as an **operating observation rather than a controlled benchmark study**.

**Not known.** The value of the operating threshold is Not recorded in this edition. Where any released capacity went is Unknown.

### Where to recognize HEE

The significance of this case is not simply the speed improvement.

It illustrates that performance improvement can involve both:

1. **process efficiency**, and
2. **information design**.

The intervention changed the relationship between demand, information, system resources, and user application.

This illustrates a broader HEE question:

> **What is the organization actually consuming to produce the required information, and can the entire relationship be redesigned?**

## Case 7 — Capability enablement: internal application without external vendor cost

*Object: an infrastructure engineer’s role and the dependency on external vendors.*

### Situation

Internal technical capability existed, or could be developed, for relevant infrastructure work.

However, organizational conditions had created a pattern of external dependency.

The issue was therefore not simply whether the organization possessed technical knowledge.

The deeper question was whether existing capability could be converted into effective organizational application.

This produced an important HEE distinction:

> **Capability is potential. Capacity is the part of it available in time, attention, and energy. Enablement is whether it can be reached. Application puts it to work.**

Capability alone does not constitute available organizational capacity.

### What the author did and why

The role of an Infrastructure Engineer was expanded beyond narrow technical execution.

The operating scope included:

- understanding problems;
- designing solutions;
- implementing solutions;
- conducting health checks;
- contributing to architecture;
- supporting continuous improvement; and
- taking greater ownership of infrastructure outcomes.

The intervention therefore went beyond training.

It addressed the organizational conditions required for capability to become application. The logic was **ENABLE**.

### Reported result

Applicable infrastructure work was undertaken internally without external vendor cost.

The important change was therefore not simply the acquisition of additional knowledge.

It was the conversion of internal capability into effective application.

The logic can be represented as:

**Capability → Application → Execution**

rather than:

**Capability → Dependency → External Consumption**

**Not known.** The vendor cost avoided and the internal time spent are Not recorded in this edition, so the size of the saving is Unknown. Where any released capacity went is Unknown.

### Where to recognize HEE

This case became particularly important to the development of **Human Energy Enablement (HEEn)**.

It illustrated that an organization may possess capability while still failing to obtain the capacity that capability could provide.

The constraint may lie in organizational conditions rather than in the capability itself.

## Case 8 — Solution purchase and month-wise stocking

*Object: predictable small-device and accessory requirements.*

### Situation

Small devices and accessories were repeatedly required in predictable patterns.

However, these requirements were processed as individual procurement events.

The resulting process involved repeated:

- requests;
- approvals;
- procurement activity;
- coordination; and
- fulfillment.

The underlying demand was legitimate.

The problem was the way predictable demand was being processed.

### What the author did and why

The procurement approach was redesigned through:

- functional solution purchasing; and
- month-wise stocking.

The relevant 5R logic was:

> **RE-ENGINEER + RETAIN**

The intervention did not seek to eliminate legitimate demand.

It redesigned how that demand was satisfied.

### Reported result

The redesigned approach produced:

- faster fulfillment; and
- reduced recurring process man-hours.

The organization continued to satisfy legitimate requirements while reducing the recurring process consumption associated with satisfying them individually.

**Not known.** No figures are recorded for the speed of fulfillment or the man-hours. Whether the expenditure was justified is Unknown unless assessed. Where any released capacity went is Unknown.

### Where to recognize HEE

This case illustrates why **RETAIN** is an essential part of the 5R Cascade.

Not all recurring demand is unnecessary.

Some demand creates legitimate value and should remain.

The HEE question is therefore not:

> **How do we eliminate recurring demand?**

It is:

> **Which recurring demand should be eliminated, which should be reduced or replaced, and which should be retained but satisfied differently?**

This distinction prevents demand reduction from becoming an objective in itself.

#### Limitations and boundaries of the record

The cases:

- arise from particular organizational contexts (mostly AKG, plus two transport companies);
- do not establish universal empirical validity, or that identical results or magnitudes will occur elsewhere;
- do not establish exclusive attribution where multiple factors may have contributed;
- were designed, implemented, and assessed by the same person who originated HEE, without independent review;
- record where released capacity went only as an author report for the support cases and Case 1, and not at all for the others;
- cannot show, from a fall or a zero in request logs alone, that HEE or its frameworks caused the change, because other changes occurred and fewer requests can have other explanations;
- are not controlled or randomized experiments, do not constitute independent replication, and do not provide statistical generalizability; and
- do not represent a complete account of all work relevant to HEE.

The choices to intervene in these cases are described by the author. This record does not assess their Decision Process Quality or Decision Quality (see [The Decision Point](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-economics#the-decision-point)), and it reports outcomes as author-reported, not independently verified. A good reported outcome does not prove a sound choice, and the reverse.

The appropriate claim is narrow. The cases illustrate how concepts that became part of HEE and its frameworks were translated into practical operating interventions. They are **author-reported operating evidence and proposition-generating observations, not universal proof.** The credibility of a theory depends not only on what it claims, but also on what it refuses to claim.

**Use for review or audit.** Use these cases to generate questions, not to infer a cause or compliance finding from a reported success. A case narrative alone cannot establish conformity. An independent audit needs a stated criterion, scope, traceable evidence and appropriate authorization; no permission or research access is implied here. See the [HEE Core audit route](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-economics#diagnosis-and-audit-route-h) and the [HEEn verification guide](https://github.com/titumozammel/human-energy-enablement#verify-practice-against-a-standard).
