## Start with your context: when, what, and where?

**(Time, Object, and Location) [H]** HEE helps you look at each situation through three things: Time, Object, and Location. Then you decide which conditions to keep, change, or stop. This frame organizes your questions; it does not replace them. You still need to examine objectives, evidence, dependencies, constraints, and consequences.

*Time* is when: the timing, how often, how long, the cycle, and the window for the decision. *Object* identifies the concerns that materially matter to this inquiry: those that could reasonably change the decision, the usable capacity, or a consequential result for those affected. Many concerns may be present (a process, a drain, people, a role, an objective, a vision, a mission, a goal). Write down which ones you set aside, and why. *Location* is where: the physical setting, the culture, the conditions, and related factors.

In the author’s practice, context guides the focus and shapes the questions. Evidence can support the diagnosis (**Diagnose**), reduce decision-relevant uncertainty before a decision (**Establish**), or check practice against a standard (**Verify**). What the evidence shows informs the action. The [Decision Point](#the-decision-point) supports review before commitment and after outcomes emerge. Write one line each for Time, Object, and Location, and compare them with those of any case you read before applying its lessons. HEE applies where the Object affects capacity, demand, application, or execution. This frame is a guide, not a required sequence, and others have not yet tested it. The [3C Strategic Approach](https://github.com/titumozammel/3c-strategic-approach) is used inside this frame: its three contexts (Source, Dependency, Constraint) are lenses for looking at a condition, and they are distinct from the frame.

## HEE in four questions

**1. What is HEE?** Human Energy Economics (HEE) is a capacity lens informed by economic questions about scarcity, opportunity cost, renewal, and future value. It is a practice-driven approach: it comes from real work. This record is the companion practice record. It has eight examples from the author’s experience, two at transport companies and six at Abul Khair Group. HEE was not named when they happened. You are invited to recognize HEE’s questions and reasoning in them.

**2. What does HEE contribute?** HEE offers one connected way to ask three things: what limits execution, what evidence supports the answer, and whether others can review the decision and its results. It also asks two separate questions: was the capacity we used *Justified?*, and is the real demand of the next cycle *Covered?* This is a proposal. It is not yet an established finding, and it is not shown to be better than other approaches. This record adds examples from the author’s experience. They show where HEE questions can be found in real decisions, and where the evidence is Not recorded.

**3. How do I apply it to my problem?** *Context shapes the route.* Start with the problem you can see and the decision you need to make. Then choose and combine three routes: **Diagnose** (look for possible causes), **Establish** (collect the evidence you need for a sound conclusion), and **Verify** (compare what is done with a stated standard). The routes work together; you do not have to follow them in order. Change the route when the evidence or the conditions change. Every route depends on context and evidence. Use this document to see HEE questions in real operating decisions.

**4. How should I use HEE?** Use HEE to ask better questions and to review decisions with your own evidence and judgment. It supports your thinking; it does not replace expert judgment. Read these cases as examples from the author’s experience. The author designed, carried out, and assessed the changes, and also originated HEE, so the cases are not independent evidence or controlled research. ## 1. CONTEXT

Human Energy Economics (HEE) developed from a central practical observation: organizations can lose execution capacity not only because they lack people, skills, technology, or resources, but because existing organizational capacity is repeatedly consumed by unnecessary, preventable, poorly designed, or unnecessarily human-mediated work.

In the author's role as Chief Innovation Officer (CInO) at **Abul Khair Group (AKG)**, a diversified Bangladeshi business group, a number of technology and operating interventions were designed and implemented to address recurring operational demand, internal capability, external dependency, process design, prevention, self-service, and organizational enablement.

**Official website:** [Abul Khair Group](https://www.abulkhairgroup.com/)

These interventions were not originally designed as demonstrations of HEE. They were practical responses to operating problems.

Cases 1 and 2 come from two other organizations, RR Transport (2017–2018) and Bismillah Transport (2018–2019), transport and logistics companies where the author served as acting CEO during an independent advisory period. They pre-date the AKG work and the naming of HEE, and are read through HEE in retrospect. They are included to show HEE outside IT and outside AKG.

Over time, however, repeated patterns became visible.

The practical question increasingly shifted from:

> **How can we make existing work faster?**

to:

> **Why does this work continue to consume organizational capacity at all?**

This shift produced a different intervention logic.

Instead of only processing demand more efficiently, interventions increasingly sought to:

- remove unnecessary recurring demand;
- reduce avoidable activity;
- replace human-mediated work with controlled alternatives;
- re-engineer recurring processes and operating conditions;
- prevent recurring problems rather than repeatedly respond to them;
- develop and enable internal capability;
- redesign predictable demand;
- reduce unnecessary external dependency; and
- convert capability into effective application.

The central practical idea became simple:

> **Unnecessary drain is stopped; legitimate demand is redesigned.**

Reduced recurring work may create an opportunity to release capacity. This record does not establish the amount of usable capacity released, its subsequent use, or future coverage.

## 3. CENTRAL HEE PRACTICAL LOGIC

HEE examines not only organizational output, but also the organizational capacity consumed in producing that output.

A recurring operational pattern can be represented as:

**Recurring Operational Demand → Human Energy Consumption → Available Capacity Reduced → Higher-Value Application Constrained**

The proposed reverse logic (set out in Section 18; its later steps are follow-up objectives that this record does not assess) is:

**Identify Recurring Consumption → Diagnose Condition → Select a Disposition → Change the Condition → Preserve Capacity → Enable Application → Strengthen Execution**

This distinction is important.

> **Stopping the drain is not the same as improving the response to the drain.**

An organization may become highly efficient at processing unnecessary demand while continuing to consume Human Energy and organizational capacity on that demand.

HEE therefore asks a prior question:

> **Should the organization still be consuming this capacity in this way?**

This record applies a drain lens. The Core README adds two separate checks: whether capacity spent was **Justified?** and whether next-cycle demand is **Covered?** The cases below mainly address the first: they judge a recurring demand not warranted in its current form (Case 8 judges the demand legitimate). They do not assess whether future capacity was covered, so that question is **Not assessed** here.

### The Decision Point

*Quoted from the Core README’s The Decision Point (the canonical text, without its illustrative example). If the two differ, the Core README governs.*

Within HEE, consequential decisions along the pathway from capability to sustainable results should be explicit and reviewable. Evaluate them using four distinct definitions:

1. **Decision Enablement:** whether the necessary decision rights, information, authority, and access exist.
2. **Decision Process Quality:** how the decision was reached.
3. **Decision Quality:** whether the choice was appropriate given what could reasonably be known.
4. **Decision Outcome Quality:** what actually happened.

**What counts as a decision.** In HEE, a decision is a choice about what to do, including waiting or taking no action. A reviewable decision records its reasons, evidence, and uncertainty in proportion to the stakes. A choice made without such a record is still a decision, but HEE cannot fully assess its basis; add the reasons and evidence if you want it reviewed.

**Model boundary.** Decision Process Quality remains the core model’s conversion condition. These four definitions are complementary assessment lenses, not four additional conversion conditions or compulsory sequential stages.

Assess these separately. A good outcome does not prove that the process or choice was sound; a poor outcome does not automatically establish that either was unsound. Context shapes every decision: its Time, Object, and Location, together with the legitimate demand, available evidence, constraints, alternatives, uncertainty, and consequences.

**The Two Checks.** Apply the Two Checks proportionately to decisions affecting capacity:

- **Justified?** Was the expenditure of capacity warranted?
- **Covered?** Can credible expected usable capacity meet legitimate next-cycle demand?

A justified expenditure can still leave future capacity Not covered. Where evidence is insufficient, record Unknown and specify the next evidence request or precautionary action.

**Investigation routes.** Choose the route that fits the question:

- **Diagnose** (the Diagnostic Approach): investigate symptoms, test possible causes, and select a useful intervention.
- **Establish:** gather the evidence needed to make an uncertain question decidable.
- **Verify** (the Audit Approach): compare practice with a stated standard using traceable evidence.

These routes can inform one another; they are not compulsory sequential stages.

**The compressed conversion pathway and evidence boundaries.** Capability → Application → Execution → Value → Sustainable Growth. This compressed pathway does not replace the core model’s Human Energy, Connection, Decision Process Quality, Organizational Enablement, capacity, or renewal conditions. The Decision Point operates throughout this intended pathway. It makes choices and their consequences inspectable; it does not establish progression or sustainable results.

Customer Experience outcomes provide important evidence of customer-facing value. Sustainable Growth requires examining CX alongside employee experience, operating results, future-capacity coverage, and wider value consequences over time. Good CX alone does not establish that future delivery can be sustained.

The choices to intervene in these cases are described by the author. This record does not assess their Decision Process Quality or Decision Quality, and it reports outcomes (Decision Outcome Quality) as author-reported, not independently verified. A good reported outcome does not prove a sound choice, and the reverse.

## 4. CASE 1 — RR TRANSPORT: A BETTER QUESTION AND A BETTER PROCESS

> **About this case.** RR Transport is a transport and logistics company with 90 trucks. The fleet size shows how big the company is; it is not a result. The author was acting CEO in 2017–2018, during the author’s independent advisory work. HEE was not named then; the author summarized this experience as HEE in 2026. Readers are invited to recognize HEE’s questions and reasoning in this case. RR Transport carries AKG products. This is not an AKG case and not an IT case. It is author-reported. The figures come from the author’s published LinkedIn profile, and their baselines, periods, and measurement methods are Not recorded.

### 4.1 What the author observed

Drivers went to the customer’s warehouse. They loaded a covered van against the shipment or delivery order. Then they drove the loaded van to head office to get advance payment and a fuel slip, and to arrange maintenance. The drive from the warehouse to head office took about 45 minutes. Every step was manual. To monitor a trip, managers asked the driver, “Where are you?”

The author saw that this way of working used up capacity in many places:

- Deliveries were late, and customers were not satisfied.
- Vans used extra fuel.
- Drivers lost time and effort (human energy drain and leakage).
- Fuel records were hard to reconcile with the pump office.
- Vans broke down on the road, and roadside repair took time.
- Records were written by hand in different books. This cost time and effort, and the data were scattered.
- After loading, maintenance sometimes took days. Some goods with expiry dates were damaged because they expired while the loaded van waited.

The author describes the management before the change as poor.

### 4.2 How the author solved and handled the situation

The author changed the question first, and then the process.

**The question.** Monitoring changed from “Where are you?” to “Why are you there?” A service monitoring dashboard and a mobile application followed each job from work order to delivery. GPS tracked each vehicle. The new question needed new evidence: not only where a vehicle was, but why it was there.

**The process.** The author changed the order of the work:

1. The driver comes to the garage, at the head-office location, for a health check of the vehicle.
2. After the check, the driver goes to the warehouse and loads. Then the vehicle goes directly to the customer. The detour to the garage after loading was removed.
3. The driver takes advance money and gets the fuel slip from a manager at the fuel pump. This manager was moved from another job, not newly hired.

**Fuel and records.** The author redesigned fuel management on four principles: economy, efficiency, effectiveness, and value for money. Fuel tickets are issued at the fuel station, and only to assigned vehicles. This reduced false tickets and the gap in reconciliation. Fuel is now reconciled with the pump office every day, like a bank account. The application replaced the handwritten books with a data entry window, so records are made in one place. The need for records stayed. Only the manual writing stopped.

The relevant 5R logic was:

> **RE-ENGINEER** (with Replace of the manual books and a Reduce effect on roadside breakdowns)

The need for advance money, fuel slips, and maintenance was real and stayed. What changed was where and when each was provided.

### 4.3 The consistent result the author reports

In the author’s account, changing the question and improving the operating process raised customer satisfaction and the profitability of the business. The author reports the following (author-reported; basis Not recorded):

- profitability restored within 6 months;
- fleet utilization raised to 95–100%;
- service downtime reduced by 80%;
- fewer late deliveries, and less damage to goods, because maintenance is now done before loading;
- lower fuel cost, fewer false fuel tickets, and a smaller reconciliation gap;
- far fewer breakdowns on the road, and far less time spent on roadside repair;
- the recording drain stopped, and decisions became better informed because the data are in one place;
- easier management, because visualization made the operation visible;
- managers and drivers satisfied with the automation (the author’s observation); and
- more trips. The author reports this as the capacity gain: the driver and vehicle time that was freed went into more trips.

### 4.4 Where to recognize HEE in this case

**Context.** Time: 2017–2018, each delivery cycle. Object: the cycle from work order to delivery. Location: warehouses about 45 minutes’ drive from head office. The context shaped the question. “Where are you?” collects a location and stops. “Why are you there?” collects reasons, and reasons point to causes you can change.

The drain was in the order of the work and in the way of monitoring. It was not in the drivers or managers. The same real needs (cash, fuel slips, maintenance) were met, in a different order and place. So this case is Re-engineer, not Remove: no need was removed, and the causes that kept coming back were changed.

One data window supports what the Decision Point calls Decision Enablement: the people who decide can see current data in one place. This record cannot show that decisions improved. It says only that, in the author’s account, they became better informed.

The health check follows the pattern in Cases 3 and 5: find the problem before it becomes a request or a breakdown, instead of reacting afterwards.

**Limits.** The new question, the new order of work, the health check, fuel control, the application, and the dashboard worked together, so we cannot separate their effects. The figures are the author’s, from a turnaround with many changes, and nobody else has verified them. We did not assess whether the extra trips were justified or whether future capacity is covered.

## 5. CASE 2 — BISMILLAH TRANSPORT: SEEING PROFIT AND LOSS FOR EACH VEHICLE

> **About this case.** Bismillah Transport is a transport and logistics company with 130 trucks. The fleet size shows how big the company is; it is not a result. The author was acting CEO in 2018–2019, during the author’s independent advisory work, after the RR Transport engagement. HEE was not named then; the author summarized this experience as HEE in 2026. Readers are invited to recognize HEE’s questions and reasoning in this case. This is not an AKG case and not an IT case. It is author-reported. The figures come from the author’s published LinkedIn profile, and their baselines, periods, and measurement methods are Not recorded.

### 5.1 What the author observed

The owner looked at financial statements. They show how the whole business is doing: its performance and its position. They do not show which vehicles make money and which lose money. A vehicle that loses money every month is hidden inside the total. So the owner’s decisions about vehicles lacked this information.

The author saw that the statements answer “How is the business doing?” but cannot answer “Which vehicles cost the business more than they earn?”

### 5.2 How the author solved and handled the situation

The author built an application. It follows the idea of activity-based costing and management (ABC/M), a method the author knows by name and adapted from his own understanding of it; how closely the application matches ABC/M as published has not been checked. It is built around the vehicle:

- The chart of accounts is kept for each vehicle. Maintenance cost is recorded by part (for example engine, tyres, brakes).
- Trips are managed by customer order, so income is tied to a vehicle and an order.
- Maintenance is recorded as planned or unplanned.
- Trip cost, fuel, driver cost, and administrative expense are posted to the vehicle, together with its on-trip or off-trip status.
- Profit or loss for each vehicle is income minus expenses.

The owner makes the report for every vehicle on the first day of each month at 09:00. This is a fixed monthly review.

Nothing was removed in this case. The author gave the owner the evidence to decide. So it is an enablement case, not a drain-removal case.

> **ENABLE** (decision enablement)

### 5.3 The consistent result the author reports

In the author’s account, the vehicle-wise profit and loss improved the quality of the owner’s decisions. The owner could see which vehicles lose money every month and are a burden on the business. The financial statements did not show this. The author reports that the owner changed drivers and sold vehicles (author-reported; no counts recorded). The application also supported cost control and income tracking.

For the whole engagement, the author also reports that monthly revenue rose by 35%, and that discipline, accountability, and performance management became stronger (author-reported; baseline and period Not recorded). We do not attribute these to the vehicle-wise application alone.

### 5.4 Where to recognize HEE in this case

**Context.** Time: a monthly review, 2018–2019. Object: each vehicle’s contribution, and the owner’s decisions about vehicles. Location: a 130-truck transport company.

The Two Checks apply to each vehicle. *Justified?* asks whether a vehicle’s cost is justified by what it earns. *Covered?* asks whether the demand it serves can be met. The company-wide statements could not answer either question for a single vehicle. The vehicle-wise view could.

In the language of the Decision Point, the application gave the owner the information the owner needed, which is Decision Enablement. The owner could then state the reasons for changing a driver or selling a vehicle, and others could review them. That supports Decision Quality. This record does not check the quality of any decision.

**Limits.** No figures are recorded for the vehicles, the drivers changed, or the vehicles sold. The owner’s own reasons are not recorded. The 35% revenue figure is for the whole engagement, not for this change alone. We did not assess whether the decisions were justified or whether future capacity is covered.

## 6. CASE 3 — PERFORMANCE WIZARD

### 6.1 Operating Condition

Users experienced recurring application login-performance problems, desktop slowness, and Windows-related performance issues.

The resulting support demand generated recurring **daily support requests across the relevant problem category**.

The author reviewed three months of the IT Support service-request log, by category, to find the root causes. These included accumulated browser cache and Windows cache.

The conventional response would have been to continue receiving, diagnosing, and resolving these requests.

The intervention instead questioned whether the recurring demand could be addressed differently.

### 6.2 Intervention

A self-service **Performance Wizard** was developed to clean those causes, including browser cache and Windows cache.

The wizard runs on a schedule, and users are asked to run it and check their machine before raising a service request. This is self-service, not self-healing: the user takes a controlled action, and the system does not correct itself unaided.

The objective was to enable users to perform controlled performance-related actions themselves rather than repeatedly depend on IT personnel for routine intervention.

The primary 5R logic was:

> **REDUCE**

Recurring human-mediated support for these causes was reduced by a controlled self-service mechanism.

### 6.3 Observed Result

The author reports that the scheduled self-service wizard significantly reduced slowness-related requests (author-reported; the measurement basis is Not recorded). Other changes in the same continuing process, including the hardware changes in Case 5, may have contributed to later reductions.

The intervention therefore did not simply improve the speed of request handling.

It changed the demand structure itself.

### 6.4 HEE Interpretation

The case illustrates a central HEE principle:

> **Do not optimize the handling of unnecessary recurring demand before asking whether the demand can be removed, reduced, or replaced.**

The effect sought is not merely fewer tickets.

Reduced support work may create an opportunity to release Human Energy and organizational capacity previously consumed by repetitive support activity. Whether it did depends on how the time was reallocated, any displaced workload, maintenance costs, and subsequent use. The record documents only one reported use (Section 15); the other factors are Not recorded (see Evidence recorded).

## 13. WHAT THE CASES REVEAL ABOUT ORGANIZATIONAL DRAIN

The cases suggest that organizational drain can arise through many mechanisms.

Human Energy and organizational capacity can be consumed through:

- support calls;
- interruptions;
- reactive work;
- service chains;
- preventable incidents;
- procurement activity;
- coordination;
- external dependency;
- repeated decisions;
- waiting;
- handoffs;
- recurring activities; and
- work that could be redesigned, prevented, replaced, or removed.

This means organizational drain is not simply a function of workload volume.

Drain can also arise from the **structure, recurrence, dependency, and conditions surrounding demand**.

A recurring pattern can therefore be expressed as:

**Operational Demand → Human Energy Consumption → Capacity Reduction**

The intervention seeks to reverse the relationship:

**Diagnose Condition → Change Condition → Reduce Consumption → Preserve Capacity**

This is one of the central practical observations underlying HEE.

## 14. HEE ARCHITECTURE OBSERVED IN PRACTICE

The cases provide practical illustrations of several HEE constructs.

### 14.1 Human Energy

Human Energy is consumed through:

- support calls;
- interruptions;
- reactive work;
- coordination;
- preventable incidents;
- procurement;
- decisions;
- waiting;
- handoffs;
- external dependency; and
- repetitive recurring activity.

The relevant capacity question is not merely how much work exists, but how much Human Energy is consumed to sustain it.

### 14.2 Capability

Capability represents what people or organizations know and can potentially do.

But:

> **Capability is potential.**

Capability does not automatically become available organizational capacity.

### 14.3 Capacity

Capacity is the part of capability available in time, attention, and energy. Enablement determines whether it can be reached, and application puts it to work.

This distinction is particularly visible in the capability-enablement case.

### 14.4 Connection

Connection concerns the relationships among:

- people;
- functions;
- systems;
- information;
- requirements; and
- decisions.

Disconnects create additional consumption.

A technically simple task can therefore generate substantial organizational drain if the surrounding connections are poorly designed.

### 14.5 Enablement

Enablement concerns the organizational conditions that allow Human Energy and capability to become effective application.

Where capability exists but application is constrained, the issue is not necessarily a capability gap.

It may be an enablement gap.

### 14.6 Demand

Demand is not inherently negative.

HEE focuses particularly on **unnecessary or unnecessarily consuming recurring demand**.

The objective is not to eliminate valuable work.

It is to distinguish between:

- demand that should disappear;
- demand that should be reduced;
- demand that can be replaced;
- demand whose conditions should be re-engineered; and
- demand that should legitimately remain.

### 14.7 3C Strategic Lens

The cases also reflect the **3C Strategic Lens**:

**SOURCE · DEPENDENCY · CONSTRAINT** (three parallel lenses, not a sequence)

The purpose is to identify what generates recurring consumption, what dependency sustains it, and what condition prevents a better operating state.

### 14.8 5R Cascade

The 5R dispositions can then be compared:

**Remove, Reduce, Replace, Re-engineer, Retain**

This is an order of consideration for a recurring demand, not a ranking, a first-feasible rule, or a mandatory sequence; Retain may be appropriate at any point. The cases suggest that different operating conditions may call for different dispositions.

### 14.9 Human Energy Enablement

Where capability exists but cannot be effectively applied, **HEEn** becomes relevant.

Its practical logic is:

**Capability → Application → Execution**

### 14.10 Execution Excellence

Preserved capacity ultimately needs to be connected to organizational priorities.

This is where the broader execution logic of HEE and the **Execution Excellence System (EES)** becomes relevant.

Preserving capacity without directing it toward valuable application does not automatically create organizational value.

## 15. FROM DEMAND REDUCTION TO CAPACITY PRESERVATION

Demand reduction is not the final objective.

The deeper sequence is:

**Demand Reduction → Human Energy No Longer Consumed → Capacity Preserved → Capacity Available for Application**

This distinction matters because released capacity can simply be reabsorbed by other low-value work.

Stopping the drain therefore creates an opportunity.

It does not automatically create value.

Where capacity is released, it should be protected and deliberately redirected toward higher-value application. This record documents only two reported uses (below) and does not otherwise assess whether that happened. Examples of such application include:

- capability development;
- innovation;
- strategy;
- customer value;
- organizational improvement;
- problem prevention;
- higher-value execution; and
- growth.

Therefore:

> **Stopping organizational drain creates capacity opportunity; deliberate application converts that opportunity into value.**

For Cases 3 and 5 the author reports that the capacity gained was used to meet continuing user growth. Business operations kept growing, with new businesses, new users, and new offices, so user numbers rose in each office location, while the number of IT employees was not increased and services continued to function well. In the author’s account, IT service could not have been kept consistent without self-service and automation, and the author regards this as the result of the practice that was later named HEE. This is the author’s judgment and is not independently verified. It is use for legitimate demand, not a documented case of silent reabsorption. The size of the capacity released, service levels over the period, who owned the destination, and the separate contribution of each change are Not recorded, and no Covered? assessment was made. For the transport case (Case 1) the author reports that the freed driver and vehicle capacity went into more trips (author-reported; size Not recorded). For the other cases the destination of released capacity is **Unknown**. The Core README's reabsorption prediction (P13) is proposed and untested, and this record neither supports nor tests it.

## 17. EVOLUTION OF THE OPERATING LOGIC

Across Cases 3 to 8, the operating logic evolved through several stages, which Section 22 sets out. The shift was significant.

The function moved progressively from asking:

> **How can IT handle requests better?**

toward:

> **How can the organization require fewer requests?**

and ultimately toward:

> **How can preserved capacity be applied to more valuable work?**

This represents a shift from responding to demand toward changing the conditions that generate demand.

---

## 18. COMMON LOGIC ACROSS THE CASES

The author’s retrospective reading identifies a recurring pattern across several cases, rather than one sequence demonstrated by all eight. Steps 1 to 4 describe inquiry and changes to recurring demand where applicable; capability enablement and retention of legitimate demand need not involve drain reduction. Steps 5 to 7 describe follow-up objectives whose achievement this edition does not establish.

#### Step 1 — Identify Drain

Identify recurring consumption of Human Energy and organizational capacity; assess whether any portion constitutes drain.

#### Step 2 — Diagnose Source

Understand the condition generating or sustaining the demand.

#### Step 3 — Select a Disposition

Select the disposition the evidence supports: Remove, Reduce, Replace, Re-engineer, or Retain, singly or in combination. These are dispositions for recurring demand, distinct from the Core README’s interventions (Protect, Recover, Develop, Connect, Enable).

#### Step 4 — Stop or Reduce Drain

Change the condition that generates or sustains the demand, so that unnecessary consumption decreases or disappears where the case involves drain. Where the case concerns capability enablement or retention of legitimate demand, the change is to enable application or to redesign how the demand is met, not to reduce drain.

#### Step 5 — Preserve Capacity

Confirm that the Human Energy and organizational capacity no longer consumed by the previous activity remain available.

#### Step 6 — Enable Application

Create the conditions for preserved capacity and existing capability to become useful application.

#### Step 7 — Strengthen Execution

Direct the resulting capacity toward higher-value organizational priorities.

The Core README’s six practitioner moves are the canonical process. This common logic is a retrospective reading of the cases to which it applies, not a compulsory procedure; the steps may be taken in a different order, combined, or revisited as evidence changes, and context shapes the route.

The logic therefore goes beyond workload reduction. The pathway below is proposed; the cases do not document its later steps.

It is:

> **Drain reduction → Capacity preservation → Productive application → Stronger execution**

---

## 19. CENTRAL LESSON

The conventional management question is often:

> **How can we make people handle more?**

HEE introduces a different question:

> **What should we stop making people handle?**

This changes the management sequence.

Instead of continuously increasing the organization's ability to absorb demand:

**Identify unnecessary demand → Stop the drain → Preserve Human Energy → Protect capacity → Enable capability → Apply capacity to higher-value work → Strengthen execution**

The central practical lesson is:

> **Stopping the drain is the beginning of capacity preservation, not the end of the intervention.**

## 21. LIMITATIONS AND BOUNDARIES OF THE RECORD

This record has important limitations.

The cases:

- arise from particular organizational contexts (mostly AKG, plus two transport companies);
- do not establish universal empirical validity;
- do not establish that identical results will occur elsewhere;
- do not establish that the observed magnitudes will be replicated;
- do not establish exclusive attribution where multiple factors may have contributed;
- were designed, implemented, and assessed by the same person who originated HEE, without independent review;
- record where released capacity went only as an author report for the support cases and Case 1, and not at all for the others;
- cannot show, from a fall or a zero in request logs alone, that HEE or its frameworks caused the change, because other changes occurred and fewer requests can have other explanations;
- are not controlled or randomized experiments;
- do not constitute independent replication;
- do not provide statistical generalizability; and
- do not represent a complete account of all work relevant to HEE.

The appropriate claim is narrower.

The cases illustrate how concepts that became part of HEE and associated frameworks were translated into practical operating interventions.

They constitute:

> **author-reported operating evidence and proposition-generating observations, not universal proof.**

This distinction is deliberately maintained because the credibility of a theory depends not only on what it claims, but also on what it refuses to claim.

## 22. CAPSTONE — WHAT CHANGED

The AKG work described in Cases 3 to 8 is a continuing process, and this record was written and revised in 2026. A cumulative direction has been visible across that work:

**Request Processing → Problem Elimination → System Improvement → Capability Development → Solution Creation → Organizational Enablement**

The reported changes did not come from a single mechanism.

The reported changes involved several approaches to recurring organizational demand. Their effects on available capacity require further evidence.

This included:

- reducing unnecessary requests;
- preventing recurring problems;
- enabling users through self-service;
- developing internal capability;
- reducing external dependency;
- redesigning recurring processes;
- improving information and decision support;
- changing the conditions generating demand;
- and, beyond the reported uses of released capacity in Section 15, protecting any capacity released through intervention, which this record does not document.

The role of the function therefore became less about how much work it could absorb and increasingly about:

> **How much unnecessary work it could prevent the organization from having to absorb.**

That was a significant change in operating perspective.

## 23. CENTRAL HEE INSIGHT

> **Organizations can lose capacity without losing people.**

People may remain.

Headcount may remain.

Capability may remain.

Yet organizational capacity can decline because Human Energy is continuously consumed by:

- recurring demand;
- operational noise;
- coordination;
- preventable problems;
- dependencies;
- unnecessary activity; and
- obsolete or poorly designed work.

The HEE question is therefore:

> **What is consuming organizational capacity unnecessarily?**

The 5R Cascade provides a disposition logic:

**Remove, Reduce, Replace, Re-engineer, Retain** (an order of consideration, not a sequence)

Where capability exists but application is constrained, **HEEn** addresses the organizational conditions preventing effective application.

Preserved capacity can then be aligned to execution through **EES**.

The resulting architecture is:

**HEE → OSF → 3C + 5R → HEEn → EES → Sustainable Execution Capacity → Sustainable Business Value**

This is an architecture map showing how the frameworks relate, not a compulsory sequence. Each framework can be used directly when its question arises, and the final steps are an intended orientation, not a tested causal chain.

## 24. CONCLUSION

The cases suggest that recurring organizational demand can consume Human Energy and organizational capacity long after the original reason for that consumption has ceased to justify it.

The interventions explored different ways of changing that condition:

- eliminating unnecessary demand;
- replacing human-mediated work;
- preventing recurring problems;
- redesigning processes;
- improving information and system design;
- developing and enabling internal capability;
- reducing unnecessary dependency; and
- redesigning predictable recurring demand.

The central question is therefore not simply:

> **How can we improve the work?**

It is:

> **Should the organization still be doing this work in this form?**

The proposed practical HEE logic is the pathway set out in Section 18 (identify recurring consumption, diagnose the condition, select a disposition, change the condition, then the follow-up steps of preserving capacity, enabling application, and strengthening execution). Its later steps are not assessed in this record.

The objective is not simply less work.

The objective is **more organizational capacity available for valuable work**.

The distinction between capability and capacity remains central:

> **Capability is potential. Capacity is the part of it available in time, attention, and energy. Application puts it to work.**

And the broader principle remains:

> **Extraction depletes. Cultivation compounds.**

HEE did not begin as a theory imposed upon these cases.

The theory emerged through repeated operating observations.

Observations became interventions.

Interventions exposed patterns.

Patterns contributed to the development of HEE.

The practical operating lesson is therefore:

> **The interventions were reported to reduce or change recurring work. For the support cases (Cases 3 and 5), the author reports that the capacity gained was used to meet continuing user growth, and for Case 1 that it went into more trips; the size of the capacity released and the contribution of each change are Not recorded. For the other cases, whether capacity was released, and how it was applied, remains unknown in this record.**
